## Has this ever happened to you?

Recently, I tossed my rOtring 600 mechanical pencil into my bag and headed to a
local coffee shop. All was well, until I got to the coffee shop. I opened my bag
to retrieve my prized writing utensil, and was greeted instead with disaster:
the metal tube at the tip of my pencil was _bent_.

This actually did happen, and actually is part of the motivation for this
project. Around the same time though, I'd joined a local makerspace and was
searching for a project to get familiar with some of the tools there. So, I
figured I'd kill two birds with one stone, and try to make a case to protect my
pens and pencils from situations like this.

## Design

The design for this project was straightforward: a two-part case with two slots
for rOtring pens or pencils, magnets to hold the case together, and a lip to
align the two parts.

I iterated on the details a bit via 3D printing and made some small refinements
here and there, but I didn't significantly change the general shape of the
design from the original concept. The final design is below.

## Manufacturing

![a CNC machine milling wood](./pencil-case-in-progress.webp "Pencil case being milled.")

Manufacturing was fairly clear-cut. I machined both halves of the case from a
single piece of maple, using blue tape and super glue for workholding. The
biggest challenge I ran into was just working around the age of the Techno LC
4896 CNC router at the makerspace.

This machine is probably in the ballpark of 20 years old, which presented some
interesting quirks. Most notably, 2D and 3D adaptive toolpaths don't work,
presumably due to the specs of the control processor. I ended up just using 2D
pockets for everything.

I also ran into a problem that I would categorize as probably more of a skill
issue than anything else. The first time I tried milling out the case, I wound
up with this huge gap between the two parts, which are designed to be flush.

![Side profile of a milled wooden pencil case](./pencil-case-gap.webp "Gap between case parts.")

The culprit wound up being a discrepancy between setups in my CAM. This machine
doesn't have a tool setter, so the way to set different tool heights is to touch
off the tool on the top of the stock. I had two setups for this project: facing
and everything else. For my second setup, I'd set my stock mode in Fusion to
`From Preceding Setup` with `Continue Rest Machining` checked. I'd interpreted
this as "the stock for the current setup is whatever's left from the previous
setup".

This turned out to be true for toolpaths, but importantly, these settings do
_not_ update the stock box points, which still come from the original stock
definition. And because I used one of these points as my origin, whatever height
got faced off in my initial setup wasn't accounted for in later operations.

Fortunately, the fix for this was easy-- I just set the stock dimensions for the
second setup manually. With that out of the way, the rest of the machining went
smoothly.

## Wood Finishing is Hard, Apparently

After machining the case, I sanded the parts using progressively finer
sandpaper, up to 320 grit. At this point, I felt like I was almost done with the
project; in hindsight, I realize I was getting ahead of myself.

I chose Danish oil to finish the case, partly because I'd read it was the most
foolproof option, and partly because an oil-based finish seemed like it would be
easier to apply than a surface finish like wipe-on poly to parts like this with
small channels and crevices.

The problems I experienced with the finishing process were almost entirely due
to insufficient research. It didn't occur to me until halfway through the
finishing process-- after I'd already applied several coats of oil-- to watch a
video demonstrating proper application. Instead, I read a few written
explanations, one of which used the phrase "flood the wood", which for some
reason really stuck with me. So, for the first few coats I did exactly that: I
poured copious amounts of finish onto the parts, trying my best to flood them
with oil. Some of the aftermath is visible below.

![Parts for a wooden pencil case being finished with Danish oil](./pencil-case-finishing-setup.webp "Finishing setup.")

This mistake seemed to be more messy and annoying than anything else. After
waiting about 15 minutes for the wood to absorb the oil, I would still wipe off
any excess, so-- as far as I can tell-- these early coats didn't ruin my parts;
they just made for an unpleasant and tedious application process.

Eventually I watched a video, figured things out, and applied later coats by
dipping a clean shop rag into the oil and then wiping it onto each part, which
made for a much cleaner experience.

Between the last few coats, I also used 400 grit sandpaper and #0000 steel wool
on the outside of the case, which gave the exterior a great finish.
Unfortunately, because of the geometric complexity of the inside of the case, I
wasn't able to give it the same treatment, so the interior is noticeably rougher
than the exterior. This, in my opinion, is the case's biggest shortcoming. It's
hard to notice at a distance, but can certainly be felt, and can make the case a
bit hard to open at times. I'm definitely going to be keeping this in mind for
future projects.

Between testing out finishes, making mistakes, waiting for oil to cure in
between coats, and just generally dragging my feet, this process took me several
weeks to get to a finished product. Finally, I used CA glue to fasten magnets
into each case part. And with that, I called it done.

## Conclusion

This was a fun project. Although it's far from perfect, I use this case daily to
carry my pen and pencil. It works well enough, and I'm happy with what I
learned.
