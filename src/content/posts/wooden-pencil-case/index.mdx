## Has this ever happened to you?

Recently, I tossed my rOtring 600 mechanical pencil into my bag and headed to a
local coffee shop. All was well, until I got to the coffee shop. I opened my bag
to retrieve my prized writing utensil, and was greeted instead with disaster:
the metal tube at the tip of my pencil was _bent_.

This actually did happen, and actually is part of the motivation for this
project. Around the same time though, I'd joined a local makerspace and was
searching for a project to get familiar with some of the tools at the space. So,
I figured I'd kill two birds with one stone, and try to make a case to protect
my pencils from situations like the aforementioned.

## Design

The design for this project was straightforward: a two part case with two slots
for rOtring pens or pencils, magnets to hold the case together, and a lip to
align the two parts.

I iterated on the details a bit via 3D printing and made some small refinements
here and there, but I didn't change the general shape of the design from its
initial inception. The final design is below.

## Manufacturing

![a CNC machine milling wood](./pencil-case-in-progress.webp "Pencil case being milled.")

Manufacturing was fairly clear-cut. I machined both pieces out of a single piece
of maple, using blue tape and super glue for workholding. The biggest challenge
I ran into was just working around the age of the Techno LC 4896 CNC router at
the makerspace.

This machine is probably in the ballpark of 20 years old, which presented some
interesting quirks. Most notably, 2D and 3D adaptive toolpaths don't work,
presumably due to the specs of the control processor. I ended up just using 2D
pockets for everything.

I also ran into a problem that I would categorize as probably more of a skill
issue than anything else. The first time I tried milling out the case, I wound
up with this huge gap between the two parts of the case, which are supposed to
be flush.

![Side profile of a milled wooden pencil case](./pencil-case-gap.webp "Gap between case parts.")

The culprit wound up being a discrepancy between setups in my CAM. This machine
doesn't have a tool setter, so the way to set different tool heights is to touch
off the tool on the top of the stock. I had two setups for this project: facing
and everything else. For my second setup, I'd set my stock mode in Fusion to
`From Preceding Setup` with `Continue Rest Machining` checked. I'd interpreted
this as "the stock for the current setup is whatever's left from the previous
setup".

Apparently that is _not_ what this means; it means that the stock is literally
the same as the stock dimensions from the previous setup. In this case, that
meant whatever height got faced off in my initial setup was not getting
accounted for in later operations.

The fix for this was easy-- I just set the stock dimensions for the second setup
manually, but it tripped me up for a minute. With that out of the way, the rest
of the machining went smoothly.
