## Premise

_The Mountain in the Sea_ is a near-future sci-fi novel. In it, marine biologist
Dr. Ha Nguyen works with humanity's only sentient android (a title hotly debated
in the book) Evrim, and Mongolian mercenary drone operator Altantsetseg to
investigate what they believe to be a civilization of intelligent octopuses near
the Con Dao Archipelago off the coast of Vietnam.

Some chapters focus on two other characters whose storylines loosely entertwine
with the main plot: Rustem, a Tatar hacker contracted to find an exploit in
Evrim's connectome, and Eiko, a young Japanese man abducted in Vietnam and
forced to work onboard an AI-operated fishing vessel.

## The Vibes Are Great

Having just finished _Children of Ruin_, I honestly felt like this book could
give me intelligent octopus fatigue. But then I read the first few sentences:

> Night. District Three of the Ho Chi Minh Autonomous Trade Zone. The plastic
> awning of the café streamed with rain. Under its shelter, wreathed in kitchen
> steam and human chatter, waiters wove between tables with steaming bowls of
> soup, glasses of iced coffee, and bottles of beer. Beyond the wall of rain,
> electric motorbikes swept past like luminescent fish.

I really enjoyed the worldbuilding and atmosphere of this book. I was surprised
that it felt very cyberpunk to me. At a large scale, it seems like things in the
world have become highly fractured, with city states or "Autonomous Trade Zones"
becoming far more prevalent. The book frequently mentiones the Ho Chi Minh
Autonomous Trade Zone (HCMATZ) and the Republic of Istanbul, where Rustem often
resides. On a day-to-day (chapter-to-chapter?) scale, smaller scenes like the
following just feel great.

> The smell of fresh coffee managed to push back the scent in the lobby of gun
> oil, ozone, mold, and neglect. An overcast early light came through the
> windows, along with the salt-flesh smell of the sea. Altantsetseg indicated a
> bowl of eggs and a stack of toast near the coffee machine with a flick of her
> chin.

^ this reads like it does not in fact... feel great.

## Thoughts on Evrim

I suspect maybe this is the point of the character, but at least initially I
expected _more_ out of Evrim. For them to make some superhuman discovery, maybe.
When they were first introduced, I kind of got Jukka Sarasti vibes from Peter
Watts' _Blindsight_, and I assumed they were just going to be playing 4D chess
the whole time. There are even suspicions from the other characters about this.
For example, from Dr. Nguyen:

> _How much does Evrim know? Everything all humans know? Or just some things?
> How does that work? How smart is that brain?_

There are some characteristics of Evrim that somewhat embody the idea of a
ruthlessly efficient android on a different plane of intelligence than humans.
For example, he doesn't sleep, and there are some scenes where he analyzes video
footage with superhuman ability, but nothing more impressive than like... some
sort of machine learning classifier (TODO: is this the right term?).

## Cool Things That Actually Exist

I always enjoy when books mention interesting, unknown-to-me facts about the
real world. In this case, Dr. Nguyen and Evrim are discussing the octopus
settlement they're studying and they compare it with
[Octopolis and Octlantis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octopolis_and_Octlantis),
which I was surprised to learn are real locations off the coast of Australia
where gloomy octopuses are known to gather and reside. Wikipedia even calls them
"non-human settlements". So cool!
