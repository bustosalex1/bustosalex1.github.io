## Premise

In _The Sparrow_, the discovery of alien radio signals in 2019 at the Arecibo
Telescope in Puerto Rico leads Father Emilio Sandoz and fellow Jesuits D.W.
Yarbrough, Alan Pace, and Marc Robichaux, as well as friends George and Anne
Edwards, Jimmy Quinn, and Sofia Mendes, to embark on an interstellar journey to
the source of the signals: the planet Rakhat. On Rakhat, the party makes first
contact with two intelligent species, the Runa and the Jana'ata. Then, through a
series of small but consequential and compounding missteps, all members of the
crew--except for Emilio--perish on Rakhat and leave behind a devastating legacy.

The book alternates between chapters detailing the timeline of the mission and
chapters focusing on the aftermath once Emilio returns home to Earth and must
answer to his peers in the Society of Jesus-- Father General Vincenzo Giuliani,
John Candotti, Edward Behr, Felipe Reyes, and Johannes Voelker-- for the
disastrous outcome of the mission.

## How did we get here?

I went into this book knowing that it ended in disaster, and also the book
doesn't exactly keep this a secret. From the prologue:

> The Jesuit scientists went to learn, not to proselytize. They went so that
> they might come to know and love God's other children. They went for the
> reason Jesuits have always gone to the farthest frontiers of human
> exploration. They went _ad majorem Dei gloriam_: for the greater glory of God.
>
> They meant no harm.

The outcome of the mission is known; the mystery is _how_ things went down. So,
for fun, I've decided to speed run the series of events that cascade into the
mission's tragic finale.

1. Astronomer Jimmy Quinn discovers an alien radio signal at the Arecibo
   Telescope. Humanity comes to interpret these signals as strange but beautiful
   songs.
2. Emilio Sandoz (a friend of Jimmy's) and the Society of Jesus act quickly and
   decide to travel to the source of the signal. They assemble a team, retrofit
   a hollowed-out asteroid called _The Stella Maris_, and embark on a journey
   across interstellar space.
3. Some years later, the crew arrives at Rakhat and takes a lander to the
   surface. The lander is equipped with survival supplies, an ultralight
   aircraft for scouting, and enough fuel to make some trips back to _The Stella
   Maris_.
4. Shortly after landing, Alan Pace, one of the Jesuits on the mission, randomly
   dies (unexplained, he just does).
5. The crew uses the ultralight aircraft to find a small nearby settlement.
   There, they make first contact with the Runa, a species of nonviolent
   villagers whose livelihood involves traveling around Rakhat to harvest
   natural resources. It's clear to the crew that the Runa lack the
   technological capability to produce the signal recorded at Arecibo.
   Regardless, they are friendly and welcoming, so the crew remains in their
   village, called Kashan, and the two species exchange languages and customs.
   Emilio's primary point of contact with the Runa is a child named Askama, who
   he becomes quite close with.
6. Some time later, one of the Jesuits, D.W. Yarbrough, gets sick, and the group
   forms a plan to have Sofia and Marc take the ultralight aircraft to return to
   the lander to procure medical supplies. Due to heavy overgrowth at the
   original landing site, the ultralight crashes, so Sofia and Marc decide to
   fly the lander back to the Runa village. In doing so, they accidentally use
   up critical fuel reserves that would have allowed them to make return trips
   to _The Stella Maris_. The crew is now effectively trapped on Rakhat.
7. The crew makes contact with a member of the second intelligent species on
   Rakhat: an enterprising Jana'ata named Supaari VaGayjur. After an initial
   miscommunication, Supaari and the crew develop a working relationship, and
   Anne Edwards even becomes friends with Supaari. As a merchant, Supaari is
   particularly interested in the crew's supply of coffee beans, which he sells
   for great profit due to their unique and highly valued aroma. Supaari
   promises to take the crew to the Jana'ata city of Gayjur, where the radio
   signals originated.
8. The crew begins to plant gardens as they settle into their life on Rakhat. In
   doing so, they inadvertently introduce the Runa to agriculture, causing their
   population to swell. It's revealed that before the Runa and the Jana'ata
   evolved into sentience, the Jana'ata were a predatory species who preyed on
   the Runa. Now, styling themselves as the stewards of Rakhat, the Jana'ata
   have essentially domesticated the Runa and maintain a protective attitude
   towards them. They enforce strict population quotas, both for themselves and
   the Runa, so as to keep Rakhat's planetary ecosystem balanced.
9. While George, Jimmy, and Marc are away with Supaari visiting the nearby city
   of Gayjur, Anne Edwards and D.W. Yarbrough are killed by a rogue Jana'ata,
   called a VaHaptaa.
10. The Jana'ata government in Gayjur discovers the Runa's increased population
    numbers and sends soldiers out to cull the Runa newborns. Sofia encourages
    the Runa to resist, which results in the death of many Runa villagers, along
    with Sofia, Jimmy, and George. At this point only Marc and Emilio remain
    alive. They are taken captive by the Jana'ata soldiers. Eventually, they are
    rescued by Supaari.
11. For a while, Emilio and Marc live with Supaari in Gayjur. Supaari eventually
    offers them a procedure called _hasta'akala_. In Jana'ata society,
    _hasta'akala_ is considered an honor; Supaari is basically offering to make
    them his dependents. However, due to a mistranslation, Emilio fails to grasp
    the full meaning of the operation. The end result is described in the book:

    > All of the muscles of the palms had been carefully cut from the bones,
    > doubling the length of his fingers, and Sandoz's hands reminded John of
    > childhood Halloween skeletons.

    Although his hands have been rendered completely useless by the procedure,
    Emilio survives. Marc is less fortunate and dies from the procedure.

12. Emilio continues living with Supaari. Eventually, Supaari sells Emilio--
    effectively into slavery-- to the poet Hlavin Kitheri, the creator of the
    original songs picked up by the radio telescope at Arecibo. In exchange,
    Supaari is granted the privilege of reproductive rights (recall the
    Jana'ata's strict population control).
13. Emilio is severely mistreated by Kitheri. After some time, a second landing
    crew from Earth, sent by the United Nations, arrives at Rakhat and is led to
    Kitheri's residence by Askama, the Runa child who befriended Emilio in
    Kashan. Prior to their arrival, in a desolate and hopeless state, Emilio
    resolves to kill the next person to open the door to his cell. When Askama
    opens the door, before he recognizes her, he throws himself at her and kills
    her.

Having written all this out, I don't know if this really qualifies as a speed
run, but that's the gist of what happens. "A series of consequential accidents"
is an interesting trope to me, and I just wanted to enumerate exactly what those
accidents were that led to the book's outcome.

## So That's Why It's Called That

A central focus of the book is Emilio Sandoz's tenuous relationship with God.
After having devoted his life to the Society of Jesus and subsequently having
suffered so much and so severely, he's struggling to either renew his faith in
God or renounce his belief completely. Towards the end of the book there's a
passage:

> "So God just leaves?" John asked, angry where Emilio had been desolate.
> "Abandons creation? You're on your own, apes. Good luck!"
>
> "No. He watches. He rejoices. He weeps. He observes the moral drama of human
> life and gives meaning to it by caring passionately about us, and
> remembering."
>
> "Matthew ten, verse twenty-nine," Vincenzo Giuliani said quietly. "'Not one
> sparrow can fall to the ground without your Father knowing it.'"
>
> "But the sparrow still falls," Felipe said.

And I just like when a book mentions its own title. Or, when it becomes obvious
why a book has its particular title.

Anyway, this book was pretty good. It definitely delivered on its premise, and
"well-intentioned, basically good people losing in devastating fashion because
they have an incomplete world view" was an interesting subject to explore.
