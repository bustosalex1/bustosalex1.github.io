## Premise

In _The Sparrow_, the discovery of unmistakably alien songs at the Aricebo Radio
Telescope in Puerto Rico leads Father Emilio Sandoz, along with fellow Jesuits
D.W. Yarbrough, Alan Pace, and Mark Robichaux, as well as friends George and
Anne Edwards, Jimmy Quinn, and Sophia Mendez to embark on an interstellar
journey to the planet Rakhat. On Rakhat, the party makes first contact with two
intelligent species, the Runa and the Jana'ata, and through a series of small
but consequential errors, ends in a devastating and horrible way.

In _The Sparrow_, Father Emilio Sandoz leads fellow Jesuits D.W. Yarbrough, Alan
Pace, and Marc Robichaux, as well as friends George and Anne Edwards, Jimmy
Quinn, and Sophia Mendez to the planet of Rakhat on a first contact mission
that, through a series of small but consequential missteps, goes devastatingly
and horribly wrong.

The book alternates between chapters detailing the timeline of the actual
mission and chapters focusing on the aftermath and fallout once Emilio, the only
survivor of the mission, returns home to Earth and must answer to his peers in
the Society of Jesus-- Father General Vincenzo Giuliani, John Candotti, Edward
Behr, Felipe Reyes, and Johannes Voelker-- for the distastrous outcome of the
mission.

## How did we get here?

I went into this book knowing that it ended in disaster, and even in the book,
it's not exactly a secret. From the prologue:

> The Jesuit scientists went to learn, not to proselytize. They went so that
> they might come to know and love God's other children. They went for the
> reason Jesuits have always gone to the farthest frontiers of human
> exploration. They went _ad majorem Dei gloriam_: for the greater glory of God.
>
> They meant no harm.

So the outcome is known-- the mystery is _how_ things went down. And to that
point, I want to speed run the series of events that leads to the disaster that
is the end of the book.

1. Jimmy Quinn discovers an unmistakably alien signal at the Aricebo Telescope,
   something humanity interprets as beautiful songs.
2. Emilio Sandoz and the Society of Jesus act quickly and decide to procure the
   resources to travel to the source of the signal. They assemble a team,
   retrofit a hollowed out asteroid, and cross interstellar space.
3. Some years later, the crew arrives at Rakhat. They take a lander to the
   surface, equipped with an ultralight aircraft and enough fuel to make some
   trips back to the asteroid.
4. Alan Pace, one of the Jesuits on the mission, randomly dies (unexplained, he
   just does).
5. The crew uses the ultralight aircraft to find a nearby village. There, they
   make first contact with the Runa, a species of nonviolent villagers who
   regularly travel to harvest natural resources. It is clear to the landing
   party that the Runa are not the source of the interstellar signal; they are
   simply not technologically advanced enough.
6. One of the Jesuits, D.W. Yarbrough, gets sick, and the group forms a plan to
   use the ultralight to return to the lander to procure medical supplies. The
   ultralight crashes, so Sophia and Marc are forced to fly the lander back to
   the Runa village. In doing so, they use up critical fuel reserves that would
   have allowed them to fly back to the asteroid. The crew is now effectively
   trapped on Rakhat.

8. Settling into their life on Rakhat, the crew starts building gardens, and
   thus introduces the Runa to agriculture. In doing so, the Runa population
   starts to grow. The big reveal is that before the Runa and the Jana'ata
   evolved into sentience, the Jana'ata were a predatory species and the Runa

9. While the rest of the crew is away with Supaari visiting the nearby city of

## So That's Why It's Called That

A big focus of this book is basically how Emilio Sandoz is basically questioning
God. After having devoted his life to the Society of Jesus and having suffered
so much, he is fighting to basically continue his belief in God or renounce his
beliefs. At the end of the book there's a passage:

> "So God just leaves?" John asked, angry where Emilio had been desolate.
> "Abandons creation? You're on your own, apes. Good luck!"
>
> "No. He watches. He rejoices. He weeps. He observes the moral drama of human
> life and gives meaning to it by caring passionately about us, and
> remembering."
>
> "Matthew ten, verse twenty-nine," Vincenzo Giuliani said quitely. "'Not one
> sparrow can fall to the ground without your Father knowing it.'"
>
> "But the sparrow still falls," Felipe said.
